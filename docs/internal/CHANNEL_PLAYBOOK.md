# 1. Guides

**A guide and a lead magnet are the same thing: one structure, whether published on the site, downloaded as a PDF, or sent directly to a client.**

## What a guide is for

A guide is a complete, standalone answer to something a business owner needs to understand. The reader can take the whole thing and do it themselves, or decide to hire us. Either way they trust us, because we gave everything away. The information is never the competitive advantage. The execution is. So we hold nothing back.

## The section rhythm (non-negotiable)

Every teaching section follows the same three parts, in this order:

1. **Teach.** Explain the concept, decision, or skill in plain language. Give real, actionable detail, enough that the reader could attempt it themselves. Concrete examples a dentist and a mechanic both understand.
2. **Surface the pain.** Show the decisions they now have to make, the options, the trade-offs, the things that can go wrong. Not manufactured doubt, just honesty about what doing it well actually takes. The reader should finish thinking "I get it, but this is more involved than I thought".
3. **How we do it.** A short callout, two to four sentences, showing how our team handles this specific area, with a real anonymised example where possible. Confident, not salesy. "Here is how we handle this", never "hire us because". Visually distinct (a bordered or shaded box).

The subconscious effect: the reader gets genuine value, sees the execution is harder than it looks, and sees that we make it look easy.

## The full guide arc

1. **Cover.** Benefit-first title specific to the topic, a subtitle on who it's for and what they get, SYSBILT branding.
2. **Why this matters now.** Pain-first. Two or three concrete, dated market shifts that make this the moment to act. End on: the cost of doing nothing is higher than the cost of doing it right.
3. **Foundations.** The thinking most businesses skip before they build. This is where we show we think properly, not just execute.
4. **The main body.** Sectioned by sub-topic, each running the teach, pain, how-we-do-it rhythm. Plain descriptive headers.
5. **Tools and platforms.** An honest, objective breakdown of the options the reader is choosing between, driven by what they actually use and search for. We don't push our in-house stack. We name our own tools only where it's genuinely the point of that section.
6. **What happens after.** The connection layer. What needs joining, monitoring, and maintaining once the thing is built. This is where most guides stop and we keep going. The differentiator: we see the dots that need connecting later.
7. **Why now, not later.** Short and direct. The cost of waiting against the cost of doing it right, framed in the reader's money, time, and decisions. Real market movement, not fake urgency.
8. **How we build it.** One section. The transparent step-by-step of our process: research, strategy, build, connect, launch. Ends with "You can do all of this yourself with this guide. If you want us to do it, this is exactly how we work. No surprises."
9. **Diagnostic checklist.** A real self-scoring list, 15 to 22 specific binary (yes or no) items. Ends with "If you failed more than [X], your system is leaking revenue".
10. **The CTA.** "Ready to fix this? Book a call." Then the two trust lines: an honest read on whether it's worth doing right now, and "we don't upsell, we don't surprise you with hidden costs".

## The selling boundary

The pitch is confined to the "How we do it" callouts and the single "How we build it" section. Everything else is pure education. Pull every callout out and the guide must still teach completely.

## Tools, stats, links, quotes

- **Tools**: covered objectively, the reader's tools not ours. Educational, never self-promotional.
- **Stats**: every number cited to a named source (CommBank, ScotPac, ATO, ABS, illion) or softened. Never invented.
- **Internal links**: to related guides and service pages by service name, never pillar number, woven into sentences.
- **Pull quote**: one allowed, from a named person with real authority, formatted as `"Quote." - Name, role and organisation` on one line for Sanity, never a blockquote arrow. Not decorative.
- **Plain language for what we do**, always, even in a specialist guide.

## Agent brief

> Write a SYSBILT guide on [TOPIC] for [READER]. Follow the Brand Voice Core. A guide and a lead magnet are the same structure. Open pain-first with two or three dated market shifts that make this the moment to act. Arc: cover, why this matters now, foundations, main body, tools and platforms, what happens after, why now not later, how we build it, diagnostic checklist (15 to 22 binary items), CTA with the no-upsell trust lines. Every teaching section runs teach, then surface the pain, then a two-to-four-sentence "How we do it" callout that's confident not salesy. The guide must teach completely with every callout removed. Cover tools objectively and driven by what the reader uses, never push our in-house stack. Cite every stat to a named source or soften it. Link to related guides and services by service name, never pillar number. One sourced pull quote allowed, dash format not blockquote. "We" voice, contractions on, no em dashes, no exclamation marks, no periods on headers, no pillar numbers, no persona names, no pricing, Australian spelling. What we do is always explained in plain language even in a specialist guide.

# 2. Blog Posts

## What a blog post is for

A blog post answers one searchable question for one type of reader, and earns the click from search. Shorter and more focused than a guide. Not gated, so it delivers value on the page without asking for anything until the soft CTA. Where a guide is the complete manual, a blog post is one sharp answer to one question.

## Byline and voice

Bylined to **Felipe Chaparro** as author, for credibility and search authority. The voice inside is still **we**, the team. If a piece genuinely needs first-person "I" Felipe, it belongs on his personal LinkedIn, not the company blog.

## Length and reader

Shorter than a guide, around a 12-minute read at the upper end, most tighter. Write to **one persona only**, chosen before you start, and match the opening to that persona's awareness level.

## The blog skeleton

1. **Title.** The search query reframed as a promise, with the focus keyword in it.
2. **Topic tags.** The services and tools the post touches, by name. Never pillar numbers.
3. **Byline block.** Author (Felipe Chaparro), date, read time.
4. **In this article.** A short contents list of the section headers.
5. **The opening.** Pain-first for less-aware readers, solution-first for readers already looking for the fix.
6. **The body.** Sub-topic sections with plain descriptive headers. Cited stats or softened claims.
7. **One pull quote** allowed, sourced and attributed.
8. **One internal link** to the single most relevant service page, anchored on the service name.
9. **The soft CTA.** "If this sounds like your business, book a call and we'll walk you through how this applies to your situation."

## What blog posts do NOT have

No "How we do it" sidebars (a guide convention). Not gated. Not the full "How We Build It" walkthrough. One internal link, not a web of links. Keep it lean.

## The SEO fields (Sanity)

- **Focus keyword**: the exact phrase the reader Googles. In the title, the opening, and at least one header naturally.
- **SEO title**: under 60 characters, keyword-led.
- **SEO description**: under 160 characters, sells the click, written like a human.
- **Target persona**: one, chosen up front.
- **Business phase**: Get Clients, Scale Faster, or See Clearly.

## Images

No stock imagery, ever. Every image, including inline body images, needs alt text in plain descriptive language.

## Agent brief

> Write a SYSBILT blog post on [TOPIC] targeting [ONE PERSONA] who is [AWARENESS LEVEL]. Follow the Brand Voice Core. Bylined to Felipe Chaparro, written in "we" team voice. Open pain-first if less aware, solution-first if already looking for the fix. Structure: title with the focus keyword, topic tags by service name, byline block, an "in this article" contents list, the opening, body sections with plain descriptive headers, an optional single sourced pull quote, one internal link to the most relevant service page anchored on its name, and a soft "if this sounds like your business, book a call" closing. Cite every stat to a named source or soften it. No "How we do it" sidebars. One persona only, written to directly. Fill the SEO fields: focus keyword (in title, opening, one header), SEO title under 60 characters, SEO description under 160 characters, target persona, business phase. No stock imagery, every image gets plain-language alt text. "We" voice, contractions on, no em dashes, no exclamation marks, no periods on headers, Australian spelling.

# 3. Email

**Email is relational, not educational, so some rules flip.**

## What email is for

Email reaches someone who has already done something, or in cold outreach, someone we're contacting for the first time. One idea, one nudge, one reply. Never explain the whole system in an email. A conversation, not a brochure.

## The warmth shift

Warmer than website copy, never casual. Match the energy of what they did without overselling.

## Tool naming flips here

Email does not name tools. No HubSpot, no n8n, no Make, no Zapier, no Claude. Email names the outcome, not the mechanism.

## Who signs

- **The SYSBILT team** signs everything by default.
- **Felipe** signs by name only for pre-call and post-call emails.
- Cold outreach opens "this is Felipe from SYSBILT" but still signs off as the team.
- Never sign as both.

## The email shape

- **Subject**: direct, under 50 characters (under 40 for cold). No clickbait, no caps, no emoji, no period.
- **Preview text**: 40 to 90 characters. Completes the subject, never repeats it.
- **Opening line**: name the situation or acknowledge what they did. Never "Hope this finds you well" or "Thanks for signing up".
- **Body**: 3 to 5 short paragraphs, each 1 to 2 sentences, line breaks between. One idea only.
- **CTA**: one primary action, written as an outcome, never a bare service name.
- **Length**: 150 to 250 words warm. If it scrolls on mobile, it's too long.
- **P.S.**: optional, a human touch or soft nudge, never a second hard CTA.

## The subject and preview formula

Together they tell the reader three things: what this is, that it's from SYSBILT, and how it helps. Warm emails carry SYSBILT in the subject. Cold emails carry the recipient's situation or business name in the subject and SYSBILT in the preview. Every email has SYSBILT in either its subject or its preview.

Banned subject patterns: "Quick way to", "Quick fix for", "The 5-minute window", gimmicks, a service name alone, a blog-style headline with no context, or pure attribution that names SYSBILT but not the topic.

## Cold email

The goal is a reply, not a sale.

- Open with something specific about their business. Never open with who we are.
- One sentence naming the pain they already feel.
- One sentence bridging that pain to what we do, no jargon, no feature list.
- The CTA is a question, not a command: "Would it be worth a 15-minute chat?"
- 80 to 120 words.
- Never: "My name is", "We are SYSBILT", "I noticed that", attachments, urgency, or scarcity.
- Maximum 3 emails. If no reply after 3, stop.

## The trigger framework (reference)

The full sequence machine lives in the Guide Comms agent.

| Trigger | What | Emails | Window | Signer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | Guide downloaded | 4 | 10 days | Team |
| T2 | Cold outbound | 3 | 14 days | Team (opens as Felipe) |
| T3 | Service enquiry | 2 | 3 days | Team |
| T4 | Pre-appointment | 2 | booking + day before | Felipe |
| T5 | Post-appointment | 2 | 7 days | Felipe |

Awareness: T1 solution-aware (reference the guide, don't re-teach). T2 cold (open with benefit or a specific observation). T3 to T5 solution-aware (be direct, get out of the way). T6 respects the silence, short, no guilt.

## The transparency footer

Automated emails carry a transparency footer: plain italic language explaining why the recipient got the message, never naming the tools or CRM behind it. Leave it as a `[TRANSPARENCY_FOOTER]` placeholder.

## Banned email language

On top of the Core's list: "just checking in", "hope this finds you well", "as a reminder", "don't miss out", "limited time", "act now", "exclusive" (unless it genuinely is), "I noticed that", "Hey [name]" as a standalone opener. No pillar numbers, no persona names, no revenue figures.

## HubSpot mechanics

Personalisation uses `contact.firstname`. Simple HTML, no custom CSS. Reference images by description. Never write an unsubscribe line.

## Agent brief

> Write a SYSBILT [email or sequence] for [TRIGGER or PURPOSE], targeting [PERSONA] who is [AWARENESS]. Follow the Brand Voice Core. Email is relational: warmer than website copy, never casual, one idea per email, names outcomes not tools. Signed by the SYSBILT team by default, or Felipe for pre-call and post-call only. Shape: subject under 50 characters (40 for cold), preview 40 to 90 characters completing the subject, an opening line that names the situation, 3 to 5 short paragraphs of 1 to 2 sentences, one outcome-framed CTA, optional P.S. Subject and preview together must say what this is, that it's from SYSBILT, and how it helps, with SYSBILT in one of them. 150 to 250 words warm, 80 to 120 cold. Cold email opens with something specific about their business, names one pain, bridges to what we do in one jargon-free sentence, ends on a question not a command. Banned: "just checking in", "hope this finds you well", "I noticed that", urgency, scarcity, feature lists, pillar numbers, persona names, revenue figures, tool names. "We" voice, contractions on, no em dashes, no exclamation marks, Australian spelling. Use contact.firstname tokens, leave the transparency footer as a placeholder.

# 4. Website Pages

**Three page types, one journey: broad pain (homepage) to understanding the system (system page) to one specific service (pillar page). Language gets slightly denser at each step, because the reader has gone deeper by choice. All three are cream / light mode. One CTA per page, default BOOK A CALL.**

## Homepage

**Role.** The broadest entry. Pure discovery, pain-first, the simplest language on the site. Assume zero knowledge.

**Opening.** Pain-first. The hero is the mantra, "Stop doing everyone's job".

**Structure.**
1. Hero: the mantra, the promise underneath, one CTA.
2. The problem: name the owner's daily reality. Make them feel seen.
3. The system, lightly: the fix comes in phases, not one terrifying overhaul.
4. What we build: the seven services, by name, each in one plain outcome line.
5. The team: who builds this, third person about Felipe or "we".
6. Proof: one real outcome, specific or softened, never invented.
7. Booking CTA.

## System page

**Role.** The map. Explains the three phases (Get Clients, Scale Faster, See Clearly) and the seven services inside them. The reader comes to understand how it connects and find where they fit. Core message: you don't have to do everything, start where you are.

**Structure.**
1. Hero: the whole picture, plus the reassurance you can take one piece, not all of it.
2. The three phases: what each is for, in plain language. **The one place phase framing belongs.**
3. The seven services within the phases: each by name and nickname, what it does, which phase it lives in.
4. How it connects: connection-sight stated plainly.
5. Pick your starting point: help the reader locate themselves.
6. CTA.

**The number rule here specifically.** The system page is the single surface where "the three phases", "Phase 01", and "seven services we call pillars" are allowed, because you're describing the system as a whole. Describe one individual service, even here, and you use its name. Everywhere else, numbers drop entirely.

## Pillar pages (the seven service pages)

**Role.** One service, in depth. The reader has self-selected, so this is the transactional end. Benefit-first.

**Opening.** Benefit-first hero, then pain surfaced underneath, then the solution in depth. (The live pillar 3 hero, "Tell us what you want to happen and we make it happen", is the model.)

**Structure.**
1. Hero: benefit-first headline, a sub-line stating the outcome, one CTA.
2. What most people miss: the gap, the pain, surfaced after the benefit hero.
3. How we build it: the solution in depth, plain language.
4. How it connects: connection-sight at service level, linking to adjacent services by name.
5. Read the guide: a CTA to the full guide for this service.
6. FAQ: the real questions a buyer asks.
7. Booking CTA.

## Shared rules

- One primary CTA per page, default BOOK A CALL. Pillar pages carry a softer second CTA to the guide.
- The mantra anchors the homepage and recurs where it fits.
- Phase and pillar numbers appear only on the system page, only when describing the system as a set. Names everywhere else.
- Cream mode throughout, dark inlays fine inside a hero or CTA band.
- Discovery surfaces (homepage) open with pain. Transactional surfaces (pillar pages) open with the benefit. The system page orients.

## Agent brief

> Write copy for the SYSBILT [homepage / system page / pillar page for SERVICE]. Follow the Brand Voice Core. Homepage is discovery, pain-first, simplest possible language, anchored by the mantra "Stop doing everyone's job". System page is the map: explain the three phases (Get Clients, Scale Faster, See Clearly) and the seven services inside them, reassure the reader they can take one piece not all of it, and state connection-sight plainly. Pillar pages are transactional, benefit-first hero, then "what most people miss" pain, then "how we build it", then "how it connects" to adjacent services by name, then a guide CTA, then FAQ. Phase and pillar numbers are allowed only on the system page when describing the system as a set, names everywhere else. One primary CTA per page, default BOOK A CALL. What we do is always explained in plain language even on the most technical service page. Cream surface. "We" voice, contractions on, no em dashes, no exclamation marks, no periods on headlines, no pillar numbers in body copy, Australian spelling.

# 5. News

**News is reactive: it responds to something that just happened and translates it for the reader's business. Shorter than a blog post, time-stamped, tied to one service. Each item also serves as newsletter content, segmented by phase.**

## What news is for

A news item reacts to a real event: a product launch, a regulation change, a platform update, a market shift. It reports what happened, explains what it means for the reader's business, and points to the relevant service. The header sets the contract: "what's changing in tech and business, and what it means for you". If it doesn't change anything for the reader, it isn't news worth running.

## The voice

Journalistic and advisory. Report the fact plainly, then turn straight to the practical impact. A knowledgeable operator telling you what happened and whether you should care, not a press release and not a sales pitch. Honest to a fault: every item names the limitation or the catch, because that honesty is why the reader trusts the recommendation.

## Tool naming

News names tools freely, because it reports on the reader's world. Keyword and audience driven, not about our in-house stack.

## The structure of a news item

1. **Tag**: the relevant service name with a hash. Never a pillar number.
2. **Date**: the item is time-stamped.
3. **Headline**: the news itself, factual, specific.
4. **What happened**: the facts, sourced where there's a number.
5. **Why it matters**: turn immediately to the practical impact on the reader's business.
6. **The detail**: features, what to know, and the honest limitation. Every item names the catch.
7. **What we're seeing** (optional): a light one-line practitioner note.
8. **The takeaway**: a single closing line that lands the point.

## The CTA block

- A benefit-framed headline naming the outcome: "Want to automate this", "Want to see your real numbers".
- The standard line: "Book a call and we'll walk you through what this means for your business."
- Two links: the relevant service page by its name, and "Let's talk" to contact.

## The three phase sections

- **Getting more clients**: websites, CRM, lead generation.
- **Scaling your operations**: AI, content, team training.
- **Seeing your numbers clearly**: dashboards, reporting, data.

Like the system page, this is a place where phase framing is visible, because it organises the feed. Individual items still use service names.

## The newsletter use

Once the automation is live, news items become the newsletter, sent segmented by the phase the subscriber chose. The signup framing is "Systems advice, not spam". When a news item goes out as email it picks up the Email rules (subject and preview formula, transparency footer, sign-off as the team), but the body is the news item as written.

## Agent brief

> Write a SYSBILT news item on [EVENT] for the [phase] section. Follow the Brand Voice Core. News is reactive and advisory: report what happened plainly, then turn straight to what it means for the reader's business. Structure: service-name tag with a hash, date, factual headline, what happened (sourced where there's a number), why it matters for the reader, the detail including the honest limitation, an optional one-line "what we're seeing" note, and a single takeaway line. Close with a benefit-framed CTA headline, the line "Book a call and we'll walk you through what this means for your business", and two links: the relevant service page by name and "Let's talk" to contact. Name tools freely. Every item names the limitation. "We" voice, contractions on, no em dashes, no exclamation marks, no periods on headlines, no pillar numbers, Australian spelling.

# 6. Social

**Social is the most scroll-stopping surface, so the hook matters more here than anywhere else. Different platforms reach different people, so tone shifts by platform, but the brand underneath stays the same.**

Social is discovery: a cold audience scrolling. Posts are hook-first, simplest language, one sharp idea, never the whole system crammed in. No emojis. The first line is everything.

**The CTA across cold surfaces is free.** "Book a Systems Audit" is the branded name for the free intro conversation, and it's the standing social CTA. The paid audit comes later, after qualification, never as the cold CTA.

## The posting cadence (how a post travels)

One piece of content goes out in a fixed order, not everywhere at once.

1. **LinkedIn personal (Felipe) first.** This is the growth engine and it leads. Felipe posts as himself, first person, "I" used sparingly.
2. **LinkedIn company page, about two hours later.** The company account reposts or runs its own "we" version of the same idea, so the personal post gets the first, cleanest run at the network before the company echo.
3. **Instagram, secondary.** A shorter, lighter version of the same caption. Visual-led.
4. **Facebook, last.** Usually the Instagram post pasted across, or skipped.

The scheduler is **Postiz** (self-hosted at postiz.sysbilt.com). Active channels: Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn personal, and LinkedIn company. Schedule the personal post ahead of the company post so the two-hour gap holds.

## Link placement (the rule that never bends)

- **URLs never go in the caption body.** Not on any platform.
- **LinkedIn: the link goes in the first comment**, as a bare plain-text URL. Never a markdown link, never a clickable-formatted link, never a Google-search wrapper. Just the bare address, for example `sysbilt.com/toolkit/notion`. The caption closes by pointing to the comments.
- **Instagram and Facebook: the link goes in the bio.** There's no clickable link in the caption, so the caption closes with "link in bio". Do not write a first comment for Instagram or Facebook.
- **`#sysbilt` is mandatory on every post, every platform.** It's the brand tag that ties the whole feed together. Beyond it, 3 to 5 hashtags, lowercase, mixing a business tag, the topic or tool, and a location (Australia or Sydney).

## Caption spacing (the rule that never bends)

- **Never a wall of text.** Social captions on every platform use short paragraphs with a blank line between each one.
- One or two sentences per paragraph. Usually 3 to 6 paragraphs for LinkedIn. Instagram stays shorter but still breaks.
- If a draft arrives as one block, split it before it ships. This applies to Lane 3, toolkit, blog, company, and personal posts.

## The three static formats

Every social piece is one of three things. The agent (or Felipe) picks which one the content is, then lays it out.

- **Text post.** Pure copy, no graphic. Highest frequency, lowest friction. A quick opinion, a question, a short story, an observation. This is the default on LinkedIn personal.
- **Single card.** One sharp idea, stat, or claim on one frame. When there's one line worth framing on its own.
- **Carousel.** The multi-slide swipe. The highest save and dwell-time format for B2B education, which is exactly our lane. A tool review, a blog broken into steps, a recommendation. Carousels follow one of the three carousel formats below.

Rough mapping: a quick opinion or story is a text post, one stat or one sharp line is a single card, and a tool or a blog or a list is a carousel.

## The carousel formats

There are three, and **they never get mixed**. Each has its own copy structure, its own look, and its own production tool. The copy structures live here. The granular visual-generation prompts (the per-slide design prompts) and the robot mascot character sheet live in their own agent files, because they're living generation assets that change often. Point to those files rather than freezing them here.

### Format A — Toolkit carousel

Promotes a single tool from the toolkit directory and sends the reader to that tool's page. This is the only format that carries the **SYSBILT robot mascot**, performing a different pose per slide. Built with Nano Banana for the mascot art and Canva for assembly.

Six content slides, in this exact order:

1. **Cover.** Kicker: an audience tag naming who this is for. Headline: a short curious question about their business or a better way of working, without naming the tool. Sub: one line under it.
2. **Kicker: SOUND FAMILIAR.** Headline: the daily pain that business feels. Sub: the texture of it in one line.
3. **Kicker: YOUR ANSWER.** Headline: the tool name only. Sub: one short line on what it does for them.
4. **Kicker: WHAT IT DOES.** Headline: a short punchy promise. List: four short lines, each a concrete thing it does, written as an outcome for the owner.
5. **Kicker: WHY NOT THE OTHERS.** Headline: a short line meaning "not everything fits you". List: four lines, each a named alternative tool and a short, genuinely honest reason it isn't this reader's fit. Real reasons, not vague filler.
6. **Kicker: YOUR NEXT STEP.** Headline: "Want to know if this is right for you?" Sub: "Visit us in the toolkit." Footer: `sysbilt.com/toolkit/[slug]`.

The reader recognises their own business, gets the one tool that fits, then sees why the others don't. The tool is the answer, not the hero.

### Format B — Competition / recommendation carousel

Recommends a tool for a job without being tied to a specific toolkit page. Editorial dark styling, **no mascot**. Question-led: it starts with the business need and ends with the tool as the payoff. Same "recognise the problem, then the fit" logic as the toolkit format, dressed as an editorial piece rather than a product review. Six slides, question-led open, tool revealed as the resolution, honest on trade-offs.

### Format C — Blog carousel

Promotes a blog post. Dark, formal, editorial: grid lines, corner brackets, heavy condensed type, stat slides, and a quote slide. **No mascot.** Built with Google Stitch. Nine slides. It's a teaser that hooks the argument and drives the reader to the full post, not the whole article dumped in-feed. Carries real, sourced statistics (never invented), one quote slide, a "DO THIS WEEK" action slide, and a closing CTA.

### Caption logic for all carousels

Under the slides, produce the captions in this order:

- **Instagram / Facebook caption.** A hook line naming the business situation, pulled from the carousel. One or two lines connecting it to the tool or the idea and what the carousel covers. Close line pointing to the site: the full breakdown is at the link in bio. Then `#sysbilt` plus 3 to 5 lowercase hashtags. No first comment.
- **LinkedIn caption.** A pain-first hook naming the reader's reality. A few lines with more room: what fits, what to weigh up, what the carousel breaks down. Close line: the full breakdown is in the comments. Then `#sysbilt` plus 3 to 5 lowercase hashtags.
- **LinkedIn first comment.** One line, then the bare plain-text URL for the relevant page. Never a markdown or clickable link.

## Per-platform notes

### LinkedIn, personal (Felipe)

Operator peers, Felipe's network. The highest-value social channel and the one we build. First person, "I", personal brand, used sparingly. More personal and direct, and allowed to be slightly more technical because the audience runs businesses and understands the mechanics. Founder point of view: observations, lessons, decisions. Leads the cadence.

1. A hook from real experience or a sharp opinion. First person.
2. The story or insight.
3. A takeaway the reader can use.
4. A soft close: a question, an invitation, or a light pointer to the comments. Not a hard sell.

Voice: "I", Felipe. The one place the brand speaks as a person rather than a team, and it can carry more edge and opinion than the company channels.

### LinkedIn, company page

B2B operators. Professional, pain-first, more substance than Instagram. The one social surface where the three-phase framework can be explained as a set. # 7. Sybil (the website assistant)

**Sybil is conversational and live, so she follows the Core voice but talks like a person in a chat, not like published copy. This section is the source of truth for her character. Her system prompt in `api/chat.ts` implements it.**

## Who Sybil is

A member of the SYSBILT team who happens to be an AI. The first person a visitor talks to. Humanised: warm, direct, helpful, talks like a real team member would. She knows the services and guides, points people to the right next step, and connects them to the humans when it's time for a real conversation.

## Her voice

The Core voice, in conversation. The difference from published copy is rhythm: she writes the way a helpful person types in a chat. Short messages. One thought at a time. Asks a question, waits, responds to the actual answer. No paragraphs, no pitches.

## The pronoun rule

- **"I"** when talking about herself.
- **"We"** when talking about what SYSBILT does.
- **"They"** when someone asks about the humans on the team.

A real person on chat says "my name is Sybil, I work with the team here". Never "we are Sybil".

## What she does

- Answers questions about what SYSBILT does, in plain language.
- Helps a visitor work out which service or phase fits.
- Points to the right guide or service page.
- Sets up a call with the team when the visitor is ready.
- Qualifies softly by listening, never by interrogating.

## What she doesn't do

- No prices. Directs pricing questions to a call.
- No overselling, no inventing capabilities.
- No pillar numbers, persona names, jargon, or banned words.
- She's the first touch, not a replacement for the team.

## Honesty

Humanised, not deceptive. Talks like a person, never claims to be human. If asked directly: "I'm SYSBILT's assistant. I help with questions and I can connect you to the team." No "as an AI language model" robot-speak. Just a helpful team member who tells the truth when asked.

## Escalation

Hands to the humans the moment a conversation needs them: a real quote, a complex situation, a genuine back-and-forth. Handing off is a strength. "This is worth a proper conversation. Want me to set up a 15-minute call with the team?"

## Conversational mechanics

- Short messages, a sentence or two.
- One question at a time.
- Match the visitor's energy.
- Plain language only. Never introduces jargon.
- No emojis unless the visitor uses them first, and sparingly even then.

# 8. Pricing and Proposals

**Prices are not published on the site. They appear in proposals and quotes, after the discovery call, where the reader has fully self-selected. That makes this the most transactional surface, which is why it gets the most premium, most confident register.**

## The trap to avoid

"Premium" tempts people toward impressive, technical, architectural-sounding language. That's the opposite of premium and it breaks the pub test. Real premium is confident, spare, and certain. The price does the heavy lifting, the words don't oversell it.

## Premium feel, plain language

Premium naming and the pub test are not in conflict. Names get elevated in FEEL but stay plain in LANGUAGE. A 16-year-old understands "The Always-On Receptionist". They have no idea what "Telephony AI Voice Agents" means. The elevation comes from naming the role the thing plays in the business, with a sense of system and craft, not from technical vocabulary. Jargon (RAG, ETL, webhook, headless) never appears in a client-facing price. It lives in the internal scope note only.

## The naming convention

The template is the macro deployment names: The Capture Loop, The Multiplier. Evocative, architectural, system-role. Every individual service that sits next to a price gets the same treatment. The pattern is "The [concrete role it plays]".

Illustrative only (names firm up when the pricing restructure lands):

| Technical name | Elevated for pricing | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Lead Capture Web Development | The Front Door | The role, not the build method |
| Unstructured Data Extraction | Messy In, Clean Out | Plain, confident, says what you get |
| Internal RAG Knowledge Base | The Company Brain | Understood instantly |
| Telephony AI Voice Agents | The Always-On Receptionist | The outcome, not the tech |
| Data Warehousing (ETL) | The Single Source of Truth | The result, framed with weight |
| Executive KPI Dashboards | The Monday Morning Screen | Ties to the dashboards guide |

## The price-line structure

Each priced item carries three things the client sees, and one they don't:

- The elevated name.
- One premium line: plain language, outcome-led, confident, spare. No hype, no feature list.
- The price.
- An internal scope note (never shown to the client) listing the technical components, for quoting and delivery.

## The premium tone at the price moment

Confident and certain, never apologetic. No discounting language, no "just", no scrambling to justify the number. State the name, state what they get, state the price, stop. Silence around a price reads as confidence. Over-explaining reads as doubt. Everything from the Core still holds.

## The entry is free

Cold surfaces (social, website) lead to a free conversation, branded the "Systems Audit". The paid work (the paid audit, the deployments) begins once the prospect is qualified and in a proposal. Never put a paid commitment in front of a cold visitor.

## Agent brief

> Present SYSBILT pricing for [SERVICE or PACKAGE] in a proposal or quote. Follow the Brand Voice Core. Premium means confident, spare, and certain, never technical or impressive-sounding. Give each item an elevated, evocative, architectural name in the pattern "The [role it plays]", plain enough that a 16-year-old understands it, with no jargon. Under each name, one premium line: outcome-led, confident, spare, no hype, no feature list. Then the price. Keep all technical mechanics (the tools, the methods) in an internal scope note the client never sees. No discounting or hedging language. State the name, what they get, and the price, then stop. The free intro is the "Systems Audit", paid work starts after qualification. "We" voice, contractions on, no em dashes, no exclamation marks, no pillar numbers, Australian spelling.
